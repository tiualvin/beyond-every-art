    The Chemical Symphony of Ultramarine

    For six centuries the most expensive colour in European painting came from one valley. What made it so hard to get is the same thing that makes it so hard to replace.

    Written by

    Livia M. Calderon

    6 August 2026
    14 min read

    Share

    Specimen

    Ultramarine

    Mineral
    Lazurite
    Formula
    Na₈₋₁₀Al₆Si₆O₂₄S₂₋₄
    Chromophore
    S₃•⁻ radical anion
    Locality

    First synthesised

    Hex
    #1F3A93

    Ultramarine was never simply a blue. It was a logistics problem, a chemistry problem, and — for the painters who had to budget for it — a contractual one. The pigment came from lapis lazuli, and the lapis came from a narrow band of mines in the Kokcha valley in what is now northeastern Afghanistan. Everything else about the colour's reputation follows from that single fact of supply.

    The name Ultramarinus — beyond the sea. Not a description of the colour, but of where the material arrived from.

    Lapis is a rock rather than a mineral, and only one of its constituents is blue. Lazurite, a member of the sodalite group, carries the colour; the white flecks are calcite and the gold ones are pyrite. Grinding the rock therefore does not give you a blue powder. It gives you a greyish one, because you have ground the calcite along with everything else.

    Getting the blue out

    The workaround, described in detail by Cennino Cennini around 1400, is closer to a separation process than to grinding. The powdered rock is kneaded into a dough of wax, resin, and oil, and that dough is then worked under a weak lye solution. The lazurite particles release into the liquid; the impurities stay behind in the dough. Repeat, and each successive wash yields a paler, less saturated grade.

    Grading The first extraction was the deepest and the dearest. Later washes were sold as ultramarine ash — often good enough for a sky, never for a Virgin's robe.

    This is why contracts of the period specify ultramarine by weight and by grade, in the same breath as gold. The painter was not being asked to choose a colour. They were being told how much of a consumable the commission would pay for, and a patron who specified the first grade was making a statement about their own means as much as about the picture.

    A pigment has no fixed value. It has a value in a supply chain.

    Why the colour is that colour

    The chemistry is stranger than the economics. Lazurite's blue does not come from a metal ion, which is where most mineral colour comes from — no copper, no cobalt, no iron doing the work. It comes from a trisulfur radical anion, S₃•⁻, trapped inside the cages of an aluminosilicate framework. The framework holds the radical in a geometry it could not otherwise maintain, and that confinement is what produces the absorption in the yellow-orange part of the spectrum that leaves the blue we see.

    It also explains the pigment's one serious weakness. Acid attacks the framework; once the cage opens, the radical does not survive, and the blue greys out irreversibly. Conservators call it ultramarine sickness, and it is the reason a passage that looked stable for four hundred years can turn in a polluted atmosphere. The colour's brilliance and its fragility have the same cause.

    The prize

    In 1824 the Société d'Encouragement pour l'Industrie Nationale offered a prize for a synthetic route to the pigment. Jean-Baptiste Guimet was awarded it in 1828; Christian Gmelin arrived at a method independently at nearly the same moment. Within a few decades the most expensive colour in European painting was among the cheapest, and it has stayed that way.

    What did not change is the behaviour. Synthetic ultramarine is chemically the same compound, with the same chromophore in the same cage, and it fails in the same way under acid. The price collapsed. The material did not. That gap — between what a colour costs and what it does — is most of what makes pigments worth writing about.
